H13 vs 1.2344 vs SKD61: One Steel, Three Names — and Where They Differ

A buyer in Germany writes 1.2344 on the drawing, a toolmaker in Japan writes SKD61, and a Chinese mill quotes H13. All three are the same chromium-molybdenum-vanadium hot-work die steel family. That does not mean the certificates will match. Here is what actually differs, and how to write the requirement so nobody argues after delivery.

Why three names for one steel

Each standard system names tool steels by its own logic. The American AISI system uses a letter group plus a number — H for hot-work, 13 for the specific grade. The German DIN/EN system uses a material number plus a short symbol: 1.2344, symbol X40CrMoV5-1, which spells out roughly 0.40 % carbon, chromium, molybdenum, vanadium, 5 % chromium, 1 % vanadium. The Japanese JIS system uses SKD61 for the same family. China's GB/T 1299 calls it 4Cr5MoSiV1.

They all sit in the 5 % Cr hot-work family, hardened and tempered to roughly 48–52 HRC in service, and used for die casting dies, hot forging dies, extrusion tooling and mandrels. In day-to-day trade the names are used interchangeably. Technically, they are near-equivalent, not identical.

Read the table carefully and you see the point: the ranges overlap heavily but the windows are not the same. A heat at 0.45 % C sits inside AISI H13 but outside 1.2344. A heat at 1.70 % Mo is legal H13 and legal 4Cr5MoSiV1, but outside the 1.2344 window. Both differences are small in service, and both are enough to fail an incoming inspection written against the wrong standard.

What actually differs in practice

1. Delivery condition

The grade name says nothing about how the steel arrives. H13 is normally supplied annealed at 180–235 HBW, but pre-hardened stock (around 38–42 HRC) is also sold under the same name. If your machining plan assumes annealed stock, write the hardness range on the purchase order, not just the grade.

2. Micro-cleanliness and microstructure

Two heats of identical chemistry can behave completely differently in a die cavity. What matters is inclusion content, banding, carbide distribution and grain size. These are controlled by melting route (EAF + VD, or ESR for critical tooling) and by the rating standard you specify — for tool steels the usual ones are NADCA #207 (North American Die Casting Association), SEP 1614 (German, microscopic inclusion rating) and VDG ASA (German foundry guideline for die casting dies). A mill certificate that shows chemistry and hardness but no micro-cleanliness rating is only telling you half the story.

3. Ultrasonic quality class

For large blocks and die holders, internal soundness matters as much as chemistry. UT is specified separately — see our guide to specifying UT acceptance criteria, where GB/T 6402, ASTM A388 and EN 10228-3 are compared.

4. Which "H13" you are actually buying

In China, "H13" is used loosely for both the GB/T 1299 grade 4Cr5MoSiV1 and for the Baosteel / Baowu Special Metallurgy product that carries the H13 trade name with tighter internal control. If brand and origin matter to your process — and for high-pressure die casting they usually do — specify the mill and ask for the mill's own certificate, not a trading company's declaration.

How to write it on a drawing or PO

Instead of "material: H13", write something a supplier can be measured against:

  • Grade and standard: "Hot-work tool steel 1.2344 per DIN EN ISO 4957, or equivalent H13 per ASTM A681 — mill certificate required."
  • Delivery condition: "Annealed, 180–220 HBW."
  • Microstructure: "Inclusion rating to SEP 1614, class not worse than …; microstructure rating to NADCA #207."
  • Internal soundness: "UT to EN 10228-3, quality class 3, 100 % coverage."
  • Traceability: "Heat number stamped on each piece, matching the certificate."

Four extra lines on the drawing remove almost every dispute that arises from grade equivalence.

Frequently asked

Is 1.2344 exactly the same as H13?

They are near-equivalent, not identical. Both are 5 % Cr-Mo-V hot-work steels, but the specified composition windows differ slightly — for example AISI H13 allows up to 0.45 % C and up to 1.75 % Mo, while DIN 1.2344 is limited to 0.42 % C and 1.50 % Mo. For most tooling the difference is negligible; for incoming inspection written against one standard only, it can cause a rejection.

Can you supply H13 as ESR material?

Yes. For die casting dies and other critical tooling, specify ESR (electroslag remelting) on the order. It improves micro-cleanliness and reduces the scatter in fatigue life, at a higher cost than conventional EAF + VD material.

Which standard should I write on my drawing, H13 or 1.2344?

Write whichever your own quality system uses, and add 'or equivalent' plus the properties that matter to you — hardness, micro-cleanliness and UT class. Specifying properties rather than names is what actually controls the delivered part.
