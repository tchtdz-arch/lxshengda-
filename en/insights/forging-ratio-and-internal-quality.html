Forging Ratio: Why Reduction Decides Internal Quality

Two forgings can carry the same grade, the same heat number and the same certificate, and still behave differently — because one was forged from a small bloom and the other from a large ingot with barely enough reduction to shape it. Forging ratio is the number that describes that difference, and it is one of the few process parameters a buyer can specify and verify.

What forging ratio means

Forging ratio compares the cross-section before and after forging:

Forging ratio = original cross-sectional area ÷ final cross-sectional area

An ingot or bloom of 100 000 mm² forged down to 25 000 mm² has a ratio of 4:1. The same figure is sometimes quoted as a reduction in height during a specific operation, so when a supplier states a ratio, ask which definition is being used and whether it is the total ratio or the ratio of a single step.

What reduction actually does

  • Closes internal porosity. Ingot centreline shrinkage and gas porosity weld shut under sufficient reduction, provided the stress state is compressive and the temperature is right.
  • Breaks up segregation and coarse carbides. Alloying elements segregate during solidification; forging deforms and disperses those bands.
  • Refines grain structure when the deformation and temperature sequence is controlled, which raises both strength and toughness.
  • Creates directional grain flow that can be aligned with the service stress — the main reason a shaft is forged rather than machined from bar.

Why the centre is the difficult part

Reduction applied at the surface does not automatically reach the core. In a poorly sequenced operation, the outer layers deform while the centre barely moves — the ratio looks acceptable on paper, but the centreline remains porous and segregated. That is exactly where a rotating shaft carries maximum stress and where fatigue initiates.

The practical consequences: die design and pass sequence matter as much as the headline ratio, and inspection must target the centre. This is why ultrasonic testing is performed on the finished forging volume rather than on the surface, and why rotor specifications often call for fatigue specimens taken from the centre position.

Values are typical rather than universal; the applicable figure should be taken from the product standard or agreed with the customer.

How a buyer can verify it

  1. Ask for the ingot or bloom size and the finished dimensions — the ratio follows arithmetically. If a supplier cannot state the input size, the ratio cannot be verified.
  2. Ask for the process route: upsetting and drawing sequence, number of passes, and the forging temperature range.
  3. Ask where the test specimens come from — end prolongation, mid-radius or centre. A certificate that does not state the position cannot be compared with another.
  4. Use UT as the evidence. Reduction shows up in ultrasonic results; a forging with inadequate centre reduction is difficult to pass a demanding UT class consistently.

Common ways the number gets diluted

  • Quoting the ratio of the largest single step rather than the total reduction.
  • Forging from a size that is already close to the final section, so the nominal ratio is met but the centre never consolidates.
  • Finishing below the recommended temperature, which reduces the effective deformation without changing the arithmetic.
  • Relying on a ratio figure while skipping ultrasonic inspection of the centre region.

Frequently asked

Is a higher forging ratio always better?

Not unconditionally. Beyond a point the benefit plateaus, and excessive working at low temperature can introduce its own problems. What matters is that the ratio is sufficient for the centre of the section to consolidate, and that the deformation reaches the core — which is a matter of pass sequence, not only of arithmetic.

Can I verify the forging ratio from the certificate?

Usually not from a standard mill certificate, which reports composition and properties. The ratio lives in the process documentation: input size, pass sequence and finished dimensions. Ask for it at enquiry stage, and confirm the definition being used.

How does forging ratio relate to ultrasonic acceptance?

Adequately reduced forgings pass demanding UT classes more consistently, because porosity and segregation have been consolidated rather than redistributed. A supplier who cannot state the input size often cannot state the ratio either, and that is a reasonable signal to ask more questions.
