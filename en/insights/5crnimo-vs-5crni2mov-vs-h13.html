5CrNiMo vs 5CrNi2MoV vs H13: Choosing a Hot-Work Die Material

Three steels appear on almost every hot-forging die drawing: 5CrNiMo, 5CrNi2MoV and H13. They are not interchangeable, and the wrong choice shows up as either cracked dies or a die that softens after a few thousand strokes. This note explains what each one is good at, and how to write the requirement so the die behaves as designed.

The three families in one sentence each

  • 5CrNiMo (DIN 1.2713 / JIS SKT4 / ASTM L6) — a low-alloy, high-toughness hot-work steel for hammer dies and large blocks. Moderate hot strength, excellent resistance to cracking under impact.
  • 5CrNi2MoV (near DIN 1.2714) — the same idea with more nickel and a vanadium addition: deeper hardening and better toughness in large sections, which is why it is used for very large die blocks.
  • H13 / 4Cr5MoSiV1 (DIN 1.2344 / JIS SKD61) — a 5 % Cr hot-work steel with much higher hot strength and temper resistance, used for die inserts, extrusion tooling and die casting dies.

What actually separates them in service

Hot strength and temper resistance

This is the H13 advantage. The 5 % chromium and molybdenum content, with vanadium, gives a steel that keeps its hardness at 500–600 °C. A 5CrNiMo die in the same hot-forging application will soften faster at the die face and lose dimensional control sooner. If the die surface runs hot — thin sections, short cycle times, poor cooling — H13 is the default answer.

Toughness and crack resistance

Reverse the argument for impact. Hammer dies see violent, repeated blows; the failure mode is cracking, not softening. 5CrNiMo and 5CrNi2MoV have the lower carbon and higher nickel that make them tolerant of impact. Putting an H13 insert into a hammer die application, with the wrong hardness, is a common cause of early cracking.

Hardenability in large sections

Die blocks are thick. A steel that hardens well in a 100 mm section may not harden through a 400 mm block, and a soft core under a hard surface is a fatigue crack waiting to happen. The nickel addition in 5CrNi2MoV exists precisely for this: it lets large blocks through-harden and stay tough.

Cost

H13 costs more than 5CrNiMo, and 5CrNi2MoV sits in between. Choosing H13 for a die that only needs toughness means paying for hot strength you do not use; choosing 5CrNiMo for a die that runs hot means replacing dies more often.

How to specify it on the drawing

  • Grade plus standard: 5CrNiMo per GB/T 1299, or 1.2713 / SKT4 equivalent.
  • Delivery condition and hardness range — annealed blocks are typically 197–241 HBW; state the range you want.
  • For H13, state whether ESR is required and which micro-cleanliness rating applies (NADCA #207 or SEP 1614).
  • Ultrasonic quality class for blocks, with the standard and acceptance level named — see our UT specification guide.
  • Grain flow direction relative to the working face, if the die is directional.

Frequently asked

Can H13 replace 5CrNiMo for hammer dies?

Not directly. H13 has higher hot strength but lower toughness at the hardness levels used for hammer dies, and it is more sensitive to heat treatment. If a hammer die is being upgraded, the geometry, hardness and preheating practice usually need to change as well.

What is 5CrNi2MoV equivalent to in European standards?

It is closest to DIN 1.2714 (55NiCrMoV7), which is the large-block variant of the 5CrNiMo family. The two are near-equivalent rather than identical; for critical blocks, specify the properties you need — hardenability, toughness at the core, UT class — rather than relying on the grade name alone.

Which grade gives the longest die life?

It depends on the failure mode. For thermal fatigue and softening, H13. For impact cracking in hammer dies, 5CrNiMo or 5CrNi2MoV. Die life is usually decided by matching the grade to the failure mechanism, then controlling inclusion content and heat treatment.
