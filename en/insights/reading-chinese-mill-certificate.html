Reading a Chinese Mill Certificate: Mapping GB Grades to DIN, JIS and AISI

Chinese mill certificates are not hard to read once you know how the GB system names steel — but they are easy to misread, because a grade name tells you less than you think and 'equivalent' is a word with a wide meaning. Here is the naming logic, the common grade mappings, and five checks that catch most problems.

How the GB naming system works

Most Chinese structural and alloy grades are built the same way: carbon content in hundredths of a percent, followed by the main alloying elements.

  • 45# — nominal 0.45 % carbon. Plain carbon steel, roughly AISI 1045, DIN C45, JIS S45C.
  • 40Cr — 0.40 % carbon with chromium. Roughly AISI 5140, DIN 41Cr4 (1.7035), JIS SCr440.
  • 42CrMo — 0.42 % carbon, chromium and molybdenum. Roughly AISI 4140, DIN 42CrMo4 (1.7225), JIS SCM440.
  • 35CrMo — 0.35 % carbon Cr-Mo. Roughly AISI 4135, DIN 34CrMo4 (1.7220), JIS SCM435.
  • 30CrMo — 0.30 % carbon Cr-Mo, commonly treated as the Chinese counterpart of AISI 4130. Roughly DIN 25CrMo4 (1.7218), JIS SCM430.
  • 2Cr13 — about 0.2 % carbon, 13 % chromium martensitic stainless. Roughly AISI 420, DIN X20Cr13 (1.4021), JIS SUS420J1.

Understanding this pattern lets you sanity-check a certificate at a glance. If a heat claimed as 42CrMo shows 0.28 % C, something is wrong — regardless of what the grade column says.

The classic stumbling blocks

"Equivalent" does not mean identical

A 30CrMo heat is not a 4130 heat certified to ASTM A29. They are close enough that both are used for the same applications, and different enough that a certificate written against one may fail an audit written against the other. Where the application is safety-relevant — pressure-containing parts, rotating equipment — specify the standard, not the equivalent.

Tool steel names travel badly

In tool steels, a Chinese supplier may write H13 and mean the GB/T 1299 grade 4Cr5MoSiV1, or the Baosteel product sold under the H13 name, or a generic 5 % Cr hot-work steel. All three are sold as "H13". See H13 vs 1.2344 vs SKD61 for the composition windows and how to write the requirement.

Test standards are also in a different language

A Chinese certificate will cite GB/T 228.1 for tensile and GB/T 6402 for UT. Those map to ASTM E8/E8M and ASTM A388 respectively, but as near-equivalents with different acceptance definitions. Our UT specification guide covers the differences that matter.

Five checks that catch most problems

  1. Grade versus composition. Compare carbon and main alloy content against the grade name logic above. This one check catches transcription errors and grade substitution.
  2. Hardness units. HBW and HRC are not interchangeable and conversion is not linear. A certificate that says "hardness 220" without a unit is incomplete.
  3. Impact test temperature. Charpy values at +20 °C look far better than at −20 °C or −40 °C. Check that the test temperature on the certificate matches the one on your specification.
  4. Sampling position and orientation. Properties taken from a prolongation at the end of the forging differ from those at mid-radius or at the centre. For critical parts the position must be stated.
  5. Heat treatment record. A property certificate without the heat treatment cycle cannot be verified and cannot be reproduced. Ask for the cycle: temperatures, holding times, quench medium, tempering temperature.

What a complete documentation package looks like

For each lot, expect: chemical composition report, mechanical property report, ultrasonic inspection report (with standard and acceptance level), heat treatment record, and — for tool steels — a metallographic report. All of them traceable to the same heat number, which is stamped on the parts.

Frequently asked

Is 30CrMo the same as 4130?

It is the closest Chinese equivalent and is used interchangeably in many applications, but they are certified to different standards (GB/T 3077 versus ASTM A29/A29M) with slightly different composition windows. For pressure-containing or safety-relevant parts, specify the standard you need certified.

What does the '#' in 45# mean?

It simply marks a plain carbon steel grade, read as '45 steel' — nominal 0.45 % carbon. It is not a special symbol beyond that.

Can you supply certificates in English?

Yes. Chemical composition, mechanical property, UT and metallographic reports are issued in English (with Chinese originals where required), and third-party inspection reports from SGS, BV or TUV can be arranged.
